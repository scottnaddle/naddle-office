# Business slide brief guidance

## Start from the decision your audience must make

A business deck is not a place to pile up explanation. It is a place to help the
people in the room reach a judgement. Before you open a slide editor, write the
decision you are asking for in one sentence. If the decision is vague, even a
polished deck loses its direction.

Think about the seniority and background of the audience first. For a short
executive briefing, lead with the conclusion and back it up with evidence. For a
board or another shared decision, surface the issues and the risks, and state
the decision you are requesting clearly at the end.

## Build the story as situation, complication, resolution

The spine of a business report has three steps.

- Situation: state what is true now, briefly. Do not repeat background the
  audience already knows at length.
- Complication: show what has changed or what is at risk. This is why the report
  exists.
- Resolution: state the recommendation, the proposal or the request, whichever
  fits this report, and say what the audience should do next.

Each slide carries one step of that flow. If the flow is hard to see, reorder
the slides. When a single slide tries to state both the situation and the
resolution, it usually works better as two slides.

## Give every slide a one-line "SO WHAT"

Every slide needs one line of conclusion. Write the thing the audience should
take from this slide as a single "SO WHAT" sentence. If you cannot write that
sentence, cut the slide or merge it into another.

The title should carry that conclusion, not restate the topic. "3Q revenue beat
target by 8%" is stronger than "Revenue status". Read only the titles in order
and check that the argument still holds. If the titles read as a list of topics,
rewrite them as a chain of takeaways.

## Name a source for every number and every claim

Put a source next to every number and every claim. If the source is not ready
yet, leave an explicit placeholder such as "source to confirm" instead of
inventing one. Do not make up figures from memory or rough guesses, and do not
add an unsupported citation.

Keep the reference point and the unit consistent across the deck so the same
number does not appear differently on two slides.

## Pack the argument, not empty space

A business deck is where the argument accumulates, not where empty space is
decorated. Give each slide one claim and place the evidence and the numbers that
support it alongside. Do not add space for decoration; organise the information
densely so the audience can compare at a glance.

Not everything belongs in the main body. Move material you will only need for
questions, or that leads away from the argument, into an appendix.

## This is a talk deck, not a document

This is a screen for a talk, not a document to read. Do not paste a transcript
onto the slides. Keep the point on screen and explain it out loud.

The visual design template is chosen separately from this brief. The brief sets
the content and the order; the design template decides how it looks. The two are
independent.

## Rehearse against the clock and cut

Practise out loud until the deck fits the time you have. Decide in advance which
slides you will cut if time runs short, then rehearse the shortened version.
Keep the conclusion and the decision you are requesting even when you cut. End
on the conclusion and next steps, not on a closing pleasantry or a blank screen.
