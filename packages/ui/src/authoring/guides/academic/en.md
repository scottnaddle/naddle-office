# Academic slide brief guidance

## Start with the argument, not the slides

Write the point of the talk in one sentence before you open a slide editor. If
you cannot state it plainly, the deck is not ready. Then choose the single
argument the available time can support. A short conference talk carries one
claim well; a longer seminar can carry a claim plus a careful discussion of
limits and alternatives.

Keep the research question visible early. State it explicitly near the front,
then show how every section answers it or narrows it.

## Titles that carry the message

A title should be a complete takeaway, not a topic label. Compare "Results"
with "The new measure tracks recovery two days earlier". The second title tells
the audience what to conclude.

Run the ghost-deck check: read only the titles, in order, and see whether the
argument still stands. If the titles form a list of topics rather than a chain
of claims, rewrite them. Keep a title short enough to read at a glance; put the
supporting detail in the body.

## Choose a narrative shape

Useful shapes include:

- Context, then the gap in current knowledge, then the approach, then the
  findings, then what they mean.
- Lead with the answer for a short talk or a senior audience, then justify it
  with the evidence.
- For a defense or grant briefing, follow the decision the audience must make:
  question, evidence, contribution, plan, risk.

One slide should do one job. If a slide both motivates the work and reports a
result, split it. Do not spend slides on material the audience will not need in
the room; keep spare detail for an appendix.

## Exhibits and figures

Give each results slide one exhibit, not a wall of numbers. Annotate the part
of the figure that supports the claim, so the audience does not have to hunt
for it in a caption.

Show evidence first, then interpretation. For left-to-right reading, place the
figure toward the left and the reading notes toward the right; mirror the
layout for right-to-left languages.

Rebuild a figure for the screen instead of pasting a paper illustration at
postage-stamp size. Use the space to enlarge labels, simplify gridlines and
highlight the comparison that matters. If you will not discuss an exhibit, cut
it. If you need it only for a question, move it to the appendix.

## Citations

Cite borrowed claims, figures and data on the slide where they appear. A short
author-year note is usually enough; keep the full reference for the references
slide. Do not rely on the audience remembering a citation from an earlier
slide.

End the main deck on the conclusions, not on a generic closing slide. The
conclusions slide stays up during questions and gives the audience the answer
to take away. A references slide and an appendix can follow it.

## A reliable deck architecture

A common order for academic talks:

1. Title: talk title, authors, affiliations, date.
2. Motivation: the problem and why it matters.
3. Research question: the exact question the talk answers.
4. Methods: only the detail needed to judge the evidence.
5. Results: one claim and one exhibit at a time.
6. Discussion: limits, alternatives and what would change the conclusion.
7. Conclusions: the answer, its importance and the next step.
8. Contact or next step: how to follow up.
9. References: full citations.
10. Appendix: backup exhibits for questions.

Add related work before methods when the audience needs to see the gap, and add
a contributions section for a thesis defense. A grant briefing usually adds
aims, work plan, team, impact, budget and risks before the conclusions.

## Timing budgets

Plan the body slides and rehearse with a timer. These are starting points, not
rules:

- A 10-minute talk: about 8–10 body slides.
- A 15-minute talk: about 12–14 body slides.
- A 20-minute talk: about 15–18 body slides.
- A 45-minute seminar: about 30–40 body slides.

For a thesis defense, a master's talk often runs 20–30 minutes with roughly
15–22 body slides; a doctoral talk often runs 30–45 minutes with roughly 20–30
body slides, plus questions. For a grant briefing, 10–15 minutes usually means
8–14 body slides, and 20 minutes usually means 12–18.

Count title, references and appendix outside the body. Decide in advance which
slides you will cut if time runs short, and practise saying the shortened
version out loud.

## Wording, type and accessibility

Keep body text light. A slide is a visual aid, not a transcript. Use complete
sentences when the sentence is the point; use short phrases when the figure is
the point. Remove words that do not change the meaning.

Use a readable type size throughout, keep strong contrast between text and
background, and do not encode meaning in colour alone. Label lines directly
where possible, and make sure the smallest annotation is still legible from the
back of the room.

## Rehearse and revise

Rehearse under time, then cut. Ask a colleague to read only the titles and tell
you the argument back. If they cannot, the titles or the order need work. Leave
room for questions and keep the conclusions visible while you answer.
